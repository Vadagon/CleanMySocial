Somewhere along the way, your Facebook friend count stopped being a list of friends and became an audience — 600, 900, 1,400 people, most of whom you couldn't pick out of a lineup. That's not just clutter. Every one of those accounts holds a standing pass to your personal life. Here's what a bloated friends list actually costs, and why trimming it is one of the highest-value privacy moves you can make.

## "Friends" is a permission level, not a compliment

Everything you post as **Friends** — the default for most people — is visible to every single account on that list. Not the 20 people you had in mind while posting. All of them:

- The stranger you accepted in 2011 to be polite
- The guy from a job you left three careers ago
- The friend-of-a-friend from one wedding
- Whoever is *currently controlling* any of those accounts

Your photos, your kids' photos, your vacation dates, your life events, your check-ins — the audience for all of it is defined by your worst friend-request decision of the last fifteen years.

## The risks, concretely

**Hacked friend accounts inherit your trust.** When any of your 900 friends gets their account compromised, the attacker sees everything you share with friends — and can message you as someone you trust. The classic scams ("I'm stranded, send money", phishing links, crypto pitches) work precisely because they arrive from a friend. More friends, more attack surface; dormant accounts are the likeliest to be quietly taken over, and they're exactly the ones bloating your list.

**Social engineering raw material.** Your friends-only content answers security questions (pet names, hometown, mother's maiden name), reveals when your house is empty, and maps your family. Identity-theft and burglary cases have been built from less. A curated list doesn't eliminate this; it shrinks who can collect it from "basically anyone" to "people I actually know."

**Fake and duplicate accounts.** Meta removes fake accounts by the billions per year; some fraction of a big friends list is bots and cloned profiles you accepted without checking. They're not there to read your poetry.

**Context collapse.** The more sprawling the list, the more your posts reach people who know your boss, your clients, your extended family — audiences you'd never consciously address together. Most "posted something, regretted the fallout" stories are friends-list bloat stories.

## Research agrees your list is mostly strangers

Anthropologist Robin Dunbar's studies of Facebook found people can maintain roughly 150 meaningful relationships — and that of a typical large friends list, only a handful are close friends the person would turn to in a crisis. The other several hundred are, cognitively speaking, an audience of acquaintances and strangers.
